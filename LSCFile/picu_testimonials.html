Testimonials

Firsthand accounts on the establishment of the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit at Hacettepe University.

These translated and edited firsthand accounts were collected from clinicians involved in the early establishment of the unit, in response to a call circulated through the Hacettepe Pediatrics alumni network in preparation for the 40th anniversary congress. Collectively, they provide a unique qualitative perspective on the origins of pediatric intensive care in Türkiye. Click any name to read the full account.

A message posted by Dr. Benan Bayrakci in the Hacettepe Pediatrics Alumni group requested documents, recollections, and photographs related to the establishment of the Intensive Care Unit, noting that the unit founded in 1985 was the first of its kind in Türkiye.

One of our most urgent problems in oncology during those years was tumor lysis syndrome, particularly in patients with Burkitt lymphoma. Hemodialysis was not available to us, and peritoneal dialysis in patients with massive abdominal tumor burden was extremely difficult. I repeatedly told Prof. Çevik that following such patients on general pediatric wards was extremely difficult and that even a small dedicated intensive care unit was urgently needed.

After working hours, Prof. Çevik called me to the chief physician's office. “I will establish the unit,” he said. He asked me to prepare a technical report and later to draft an operational directive for the unit — a closed-model system with dedicated residents, chief residents serving as consultants, selective admission policies, and structured patient flow back to the wards. As far as I recall, these principles were fully implemented during the early months and years.

As one of the first two residents who worked in the Intensive Care Unit after its establishment, I will attempt to describe the portion of the ICU story that I personally witnessed. When we first heard that the Intensive Care Unit would be opening, we first-year residents experienced both excitement and fear.

Entering the ICU from the corridor, there were beds lined along the wall; three had ECG monitors, which at the time represented a major innovation for us. Every bed had an oxygen outlet, and two nurses worked in the unit — one nurse for every two patients, an extraordinary luxury by the standards of the era.

One of the first patients, admitted with fatal mumps myocarditis, was ultimately lost despite our desperate efforts. We conducted a literature review, sought mumps serology, and subsequently published the case (Jpn Heart J 1989;30:109–14) — my first English-language publication. I remember my ICU rotation as an extraordinarily valuable, exciting, intense, and educational experience.

In response to your call for intensive care memories, photographs, and experiences, I would like to contribute to Ali Süha's account. I remember the mumps myocarditis case very clearly. The ICU had only recently opened, and we were trying both to establish the system and to provide genuinely intensive care-level management to patients who met the criteria. Those were the first months of chief residency for Erkan Karaaslan, Funda Öztunç, Güzide Turanlı, and myself.

This patient was admitted cyanotic, tachypneic, with severely abnormal ECG findings. We called Süheyla abla for echocardiography consultation; despite the late hour, she immediately came from home, evaluated the patient, and greatly reassured us. Following this case, she provided accelerated pediatric ECG training sessions for senior residents and chief residents over the next one to two months.

As Ali mentioned, monitors had arrived at Hacettepe for the first time, and this was a major event for us. Previously, we would essentially “monitor” patients by placing our hands over their chest to feel the heartbeat.

The monitors were Turkish-made, by the Petaş brand, with two different sound tones. When two patients requiring monitoring were admitted, I would set one monitor to a low volume and the other to a higher volume, so that from the adjacent physician's room I could follow both patients' cardiac rhythms by ear and ensure patient safety accordingly. As stated in Faik abi's founding narrative, when necessary, we would also transfer patients from other wards to the intensive care unit and return them once their intensive care needs were resolved.

Intensive care memories are unforgettable. In June 1986, three children from the same family were admitted due to mushroom poisoning. They were unconscious. We were on standby. I was six months pregnant and suffering from viral pneumonia.

At around three o'clock in the morning, the urgent footsteps of Prof. Güler Kanra were heard. She stated that intravenous N-acetylcysteine was needed to save these children. Since the intravenous formulation was not available in Türkiye at the time, she was making phone calls all over the place at that hour. Meanwhile, she also ordered me to immediately take sick leave: “A doctor must be well; you are harming your baby.” Unfortunately, the patients with mushroom poisoning could not be saved. I, however, delivered a healthy baby on October 6.

It has been at least 30 years since I read that text, yet I still remember the patient being admitted with a presumed diagnosis of sepsis, and how the history of organophosphate exposure was ultimately explained by contamination of laundry hung in the garden during a neighbor's pesticide spraying of trees.

Dear Faik abi, this demonstrates both your excellence as a physician and your ability to translate it into a striking written narrative. Throughout our residency training, you remained a guiding figure for us.
